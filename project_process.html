1.1 Artist Statement

City of Fragments reinterprets the city as a shifting archive of memories rather than a fixed physical space. Using slit-scan distortion and user-generated uploads, the project reveals how urban moments stretch, fragment, and transform as they are remembered and retold.

The slit-scan engine becomes a metaphor for memory itself, being unstable, reconstructed, and shaped by time. Each scanned fragment shows the city not as a singular narrative but as layered impressions, blurred transitions, and partial traces.

Through temporal self-controlled graphic distortion and collective participation, the work invites viewers to experience the city through shifting perspectives, highlighting how technology mediates what we see, recall, and ultimately understand about the world around us.

Purpose
This project aims to create a digital space for reflection on memory and transformation. It allows users to see and hear their city from new perspectives, especially through slit-scan animation. By doing so, it uses technology not just as a tool for visual display but as a means of emotional and cultural preservation.

Target Audience

  • Hong Kong citizens, especially youth navigating change and nostalgia, as well as the experienced who want to relive the old times.
  • Artists, designers, and cultural observers interested in digital storytelling.

1.2 Project Concept & Key Inspirations

City of Fragments is a time-based, participatory artwork that treats the city as an evolving archive of memory rather than a fixed place. By reconstituting video frames through slit-scan sampling, the project turns movement and delay into visible materials: moments stretch, bleed, and overlap, forming composite images that feel both familiar and estranged. This approach mirrors how urban experience is lived: partial, layered, and contingent, where recollection does not reproduce reality but continually rewrites it. The website becomes a stage for performing memory, aligning with Winograd's (1997) argument that meaningful interaction emerges when design prioritises human experience over visible machinery. Users can choose scan behaviors, steer the line, and export their own fragments, actively shaping the image rather than passively viewing it.

The artistic value lies in how the piece reframes technical process as poetics of time. Slit-scan is not used as a visual trick, but as a language for instability and drift, qualities intrinsic to how we remember cities. Through user uploads and a shared gallery, individual traces accumulate into a collective memory wall, echoing the social fabric of Hong Kong: heterogeneous, contradictory, yet co-present. This emphasis on participation directly engages what Murray (2012) defines as interactivity as the procedural and participatory affordances of digital media working together to produce a felt sense of agency. The work invites users to engage with temporality as material, to see how technology mediates attention, and to contribute their own viewpoint to a growing chorus of fragments. In doing so, City of Fragments foregrounds participation, process, and multiplicity as central artistic values.

These above references shaped both the conceptual and technical direction of City of Fragments. From time-based image making and slit-scan art histories to interactive media practices, the project draws on approaches that treat time as a visual material and memory as an evolving construct . The inspirations guided decisions about how distortion, participation, and web-native interaction could come together to form a living archive of urban fragments.

Khronos Projector (Á. Cassinelli & M. Ishikawa, 2004) explored non-linear browsing of time within video by allowing viewers to scrub temporality spatially. Its re-mapping of time to gesture directly informed our decision to let users steer the scan line and “compose time” rather than merely play back footage. We adapted this ethos in the browser: interaction becomes a way to write memory, where spatial input shapes temporal output.

Liquid Time Series (Camille Utterback, 2000) treats motion as a generative medium, translating presence into temporal trails. The work's sensitivity to human movement encouraged us to design scan behaviors that respond to user action with visible temporal accumulation. Instead of static distortion, our slit-scan foregrounds continuity, delay, and trace. These qualities make time feel tangible and embodied.

Static No.23 — Revolve (Daniel Crooks, 2018) demonstrates a sculptural approach to time, slicing and recombining footage into elastic forms. Crooks' precise manipulation of temporal slices guided our attention to scan width, cadence, and direction, showing how micro-parameters can produce distinctly different temporal architectures. This reinforced our decision to tailor scan types per video and to treat the slit as a fine instrument for shaping urban temporality.

1.4 Project Process & Evolution

1. Research & Concept

The project started from exploring the slit scan technique we learnt in imaging science studio, while combining it with an interest in how memory of the city, Hong Kong, is never experienced as a single, stable image by us, but as something fragmented, delayed, and constantly reconstructed. Through deeper research into time-based imaging techniques, slit-scan emerged as a method that could visually translate this instability.

Rather than treating the city as a fixed document, slit-scan allowed time and movement to be stretched and layered, aligning with the idea of urban memory as subjective and incomplete. This is our primary research concept regarding this project.

The web core functions as a web-based system combining HTML video elements, canvas-based rendering, and JavaScript control logic. It involves a video playback machine and respective top bar and buttons for switching videos. This provides diversity, reflecting Hong Kong fragmented into many corners with memories created by different individuals, while encouraging user participation.

4. Additional Web Functions

The Use of JavaScript FileReader API

The FileReader API is crucial in integrating user interaction and the concept of collective memory storage. When users upload images, the FileReader allows the browser to read file data locally before sending any information to the server. This approach enables real-time previewing and processing of user-uploaded photos, allowing them to be prepared and transformed immediately within the browser environment (Codeguage, 2025).

Conceptually, this moment of “local reading” reflects the project's interest in how memories of a place are first formed at an individual level. An individual first forms an image by interpreting a message privately, then transforms it into tangible visual copies, and later shares it in the collective archive. Technically, FileReader converts image files into data URLs that can be displayed on canvas and passed into the Firebase upload system, ensuring a smooth transition from personal interaction to shared space. Therefore, in our case, FileReader equals individual memory formation while Firebase represents the collective memory building system.

From LocalStorage to Firebase

To support shared participation, the project moved from LocalStorage to Firebase. This transition allowed uploaded images to persist across sessions and be visible to all users, transforming the work from an individual interaction into a collective memory space. This setup establishes the backend infrastructure required for collective participation that we initially imagined and successfully made.

Firebase is used for two main functions (Firebase Documentation, n.d.):

  • Storage: image file uploads
  • Firestore: metadata and gallery indexing

1.7 Reflection & Takeaways

The City of Fragments evolved through continuous work on balancing initial creative ideas with technical limitations. Throughout the process, the project shifted from a visually driven experiment to a relatively complete system where user interaction became the most important goal we want to deliver.

One key takeaway was the importance of choosing web design as the medium, not just as a display platform, but as an active structure to shape the artwork, allowing users to interact and immerse themselves in it, regardless of the exhibit location. Early experiments using Processing helped clarify how slit-scan behaves in isolation. However, translating this effect into a browser environment revealed new constraints, performance limits, browser behavior, and responsiveness. These constraints were not obstacles to be removed, but factors that directly influenced design decisions, such as scan width, capture frequency, and canvas size.

Another significant learning came from persistence and participation. Initially, LocalStorage was used to ensure that we could build the system within the project's limited timeframe. However, it became clear that this approach did not fully express the project's intention of forming a shared archive. The transition to Firebase, despite the time and dedication spent, still marked a significant conceptual improvement: the work shifted from an individual, one-time experience to a collective and ever-present system. This change reinforced the idea that memory is not fixed, but continuously added and shaped by community.

The development of the image-saving function was also a meaningful moment in the project. Allowing users to export their slit-scan images transformed interaction into authorship. Each saved image became a personal fragment of the city, aligning with the project's broader aim of letting users perform and construct memory rather than passively consume it.

Artistically, the project deepened our understanding of time as a material . Slit-scan was not treated as a visual effect alone, but as a method for representing instability, delay, and distortion, which are the qualities that define how urban memories can be visually presented. The final system embraces imperfection, variation, and unpredictability, reflecting the fragmented nature of both the city's physical presence and human memory.

In a nutshell, this project highlighted the value of process-led experimentation, where technical testing, failures, and revisions actively slowly achieve the outcome we want. Rather than forcing a fixed outcome, City of Fragments allowed the system to evolve through time and effort, resulting in a work that balances technical parts with artistic purposes with creative room to explore.

2.1 What is Slit-Scan?

Principle of Slit-Scan

Slit-scan imaging is built on an essential concept in imaging science: instead of capturing a full frame in a single moment, it records only a narrow slice of the image repeatedly over time and composites these slices into a single frame.

This technique results in a temporal distortion. It transforms the image into a space-time construction where movement is stretched, compressed, or warped depending on how it changes during sampling. In digital form, slit-scan simulates principles similar to a rolling-shutter sensor, where lines are exposed at different moments, producing temporal distortion.

During the Imaging Science Studio workshop, we got some hands-on experience trying slit scan photography. The main principle is that it is a time-based technique in capturing slices of an image in repeating patterns. In our experience playing around with it during the workshop, we found that it emphasises movement. Much like the barrier grid animation workshop, slit scan photography also has various workflows. The effect can be achieved through Processing, Adobe After Effects by using a grayscale gradient as a time displacement map, or even through TouchDesigner.

How Do We Utilise this Technique?

In our project, this technique is recreated using Javascript by continuously sampling thin pixel strips and rebuilding the frame based on user cursor movement. The result visually expresses memory as unstable, layered, and time-dependent, aligning with our theme of recalling the city not as a fixed moment, but as a shifting and fragmented temporal experience. The temporal distortion reflects our theme of fragmented memories. The slit scan technique serves as a visual language that allows users to express the subjective and personal ways they perceive urban space.

2.2 Early Prototypes

First Tests on Processing

import processing.video.*;
  
  Movie video;
  PImage buffer;
  int currentX = 720 - 8;   // Start at the right edge for a scanWidth of 8
  int dx = 2;       
  int scanWidth = 8;       
  boolean scanning = false;
  
  void setup() {
    size(720, 540);
    video = new Movie(this, "IMG_8770.MP4");
    video.loop();
    video.volume(0);
    buffer = createImage(720, 540, ARGB);
    buffer.loadPixels();
    for (int i = 0; i < buffer.pixels.length; i++) buffer.pixels[i] = color(0, 0);
    buffer.updatePixels();
  }
  
  void draw() {
    background(0);
  
    if (video.available()) video.read();
  
    image(video, 0, 0, 720, 540);
  
    // Scan vertical strip from right to left
    if (scanning) {
      buffer.loadPixels();
      video.loadPixels();
      for (int sx = 0; sx < scanWidth; sx++) {
        int x = (currentX + sx) % 720;
        for (int y = 0; y < 540; y++) {
          int idx = y * 720 + x;
          buffer.pixels[idx] = color(
            red(video.pixels[idx]),
            green(video.pixels[idx]),
            blue(video.pixels[idx]),
            255
          );
        }
      }
      buffer.updatePixels();
    }
  
    image(buffer, 0, 0, 720, 540);
  
    // Draw thick vertical scan line moving from right to left
    noFill();
    stroke(255, 0, 0, 180);
    strokeWeight(scanWidth);
    int linePos = currentX + scanWidth / 2;
    line(linePos, 0, linePos, 540);
  
    // Move scan line from right to left
    currentX -= dx;
    if (currentX < 0) currentX = 720 - scanWidth; // Loop back to right edge
  }
  
  void mousePressed() {
    scanning = true;
  }
  void mouseReleased() {
    scanning = false;
  }
  
  void movieEvent(Movie m) {
    m.read();
    if (m.time() <= 0.05) {
      buffer = createImage(720, 540, ARGB);
      buffer.loadPixels();
      for (int i = 0; i < buffer.pixels.length; i++) buffer.pixels[i] = color(0, 0);
      buffer.updatePixels();
      currentX = 720 - scanWidth; // Reset to right edge
    }
  }

2.3 Refined Slit-Scan

Implementation Plan

Building on the p5.js prototype, the slit-scan logic was translated into JavaScript and adapted for real-time use within the project website.

Pixel Sampling from Video Frames

Every animation frame, a thin strip of pixels in the current frame is copied into the canvas. For vertical scans, it samples a narrow column of pixels and pastes it at 'currentX'. If the scan is horizontal, it samples a narrow row of pixels and draws it as a horizontal strip at 'currentY'.

Vertical Slits

If scan type is moving:

  • 'currentX' is animated left or right by 'dx' on each frame
  • Scan line leaves behind a temporal trail vertically

If scan type is fixed:

  • 'currentX' follows cursor position when pressed
  • User can choose to either stay stationary or paint time slices by dragging mouse horizontally

Horizontal Slits

If scan type is moving:

  • 'currentY' is animated up or down by 'dx' on each frame
  • Scan line leaves behind a temporal trail horizontally

If scan type is fixed:

  • 'currentY' follows cursor position when pressed
  • User can choose to either stay stationary or paint time slices by dragging mouse vertically

The code then converts 'currentX' or 'currentY' into video coordinates. A new slice is drawn each frame, so as user enables scanning, the canvas gradually fills with strips captured at different times, producing the desired time distortion effect.

Scan Specifications

  • desired time distortion effect.
  • The slit-scan we want to implement has a 'scanWidth' of 8 pixels, the height is the full canvas height. The directional movement is determined by 'dx', which is how many pixels the scan line moves per frame if the scan type is not fixed. There are total of 4 scan types we incorporated onto our set of videos.

Performance Limits

  • 'drawImage' is fast, but a bigger canvas size and narrow scanWidth means more draws per frame → e.g. slower on mobile view
  • Captures can only happen every 16ms (once per frame) to avoid overload
  • High-speed scan risks blurred video

2.4 Website Implementation

Mouse Interaction

We experimented with 2 different directions in mouse interaction. It was either the sole purpose of the mouse is to press and toggle the scan effect, or there could be the addition of movement control if the scan line were to follow cursor movement. Allowing the scan line to follow the cursor introduced a rich layer of interactivity, where users could explore and gain more control in the slit-scan image they create. We went in this direction because this design supports the goal of allowing the user to become the performer in this work. We want to give them as much customization as possible. The user can compose and explore temporal imaging with different scan types, cursor movement and toggling.

Key Slit-Scan Parameters

Parameter Description
scanWidth
  • Default at 8px
  • Represents the width of each strip sampled from the videos
  • Narrower values sharpen temporal layers, but can strain webpage performance
dx
  • Default at 2 pixels per frame
  • Step distance between consecutive slits
  • Smaller steps means denser trails
  • Larger gaps means bigger time jumps
scanType
  • 4 Main Scan Types
    • Moving Vertical 'moving-vertical'
    • Moving Horizontal 'moving-horizontal'
    • Fixed-Vertical 'fixed-vertical'
    • Fixed-Horizontal 'fixed-horizontal'
  • Fixed types follow cursor position when pressed
  • Moving types animate automatically across the canvas
  • Some videos may have specifications for directional movement
captureInterval
  • Controls the frequency of frame sampling
  • Balances visual quality with performance optimization
  • Default at every frame (16ms) by setting limits
  • Prevents overload that may crash browser
  • Maintain smooth trails

2.6 Challenges in Making Process

During development, several technical and design challenges emerged while translating the slit-scan technique from prototype to a real-time, web-based system. One key issue was performance limitation, as continuous pixel sampling and canvas redraws placed strain on the browser, especially on lower-end devices. This required careful balancing between scan width, sampling frequency, and canvas resolution to maintain smooth interaction without sacrificing visual clarity.

Another challenge involved browser behaviour and user interaction consistency. Different browsers handle video playback, autoplay policies, and canvas rendering differently, which occasionally caused unexpected delays or visual inconsistencies. Additional testing and fallback logic were necessary to ensure the slit-scan effect behaved reliably across platforms.

2.7 Summary

The slit-scan technique acts as a visual language for City of Fragments because…

  • It visually represents memory as unstable and stretched over time.
  • It transforms static images of Hong Kong into time-responsive visuals.
  • It creates a sensory experience where time feels elastic.
  • It lets users participate in forming the distorted image.

The science behind the effect becomes part of the storytelling, showing the fact that the city is viewed not as a fixed object but as a constantly shifting temporal landscape.
